Section 1

Why Do Physicists Want Straight Lines?

About 4 minutes

Imagine you want to measure g, the acceleration of a falling object. You can't measure g directly with a meter stick or a stopwatch. But you can measure distance and time.

The trick is to graph those measurements so the data makes a straight line. A straight line has two features that are easy to read and use:

Slope
How steep the line is: rise ÷ run. A straight line has the same slope everywhere.
Vertical intercept
Where the line crosses the vertical axis.

A curve doesn't have one slope. It's steeper in some places than others. So when data curves, we linearize it: we change what we graph until the data makes a straight line.

Now linearize it

For a ball dropped from rest, distance depends on time squared (you'll see the equation in Section 3). So let's graph d vs. t² instead of d vs. t. Watch the dots on the graph above.

The straight-line equation

Every straight-line graph can be written in this form:

y = slope · x + b
y
The quantity on the vertical axis
x
The quantity on the horizontal axis
slope
How steep the line is
b
The vertical intercept
Why write out the word "slope"? In math class you may have used a letter for slope. In this physics class we always write out the word slope, even inside equations, because letters like m already mean mass or meters. You'll see in Section 3 how mixing them up can cause real confusion.

For our falling-ball graph, the graph equation is:

d = slope · t² + b

Use the equation to predict the shape

You don't always need data to know the shape. The equation tells you. Look at how the vertical-axis variable depends on the horizontal-axis variable:

  • Is it on the bottom of a fraction (squared or not)? → Inverse
  • Is the horizontal variable squared? → Squared
  • Is it under a square root? (Or is the vertical variable squared?) → Square root
  • Is it just multiplied by constants (nothing squared)? → Linear
  • Is it not in the equation at all? → Horizontal (no relationship)

You may not have learned all of these equations yet. That's fine: you only need to look at how the two graphed variables are connected.

Section 3

What Does the Slope Mean? Let the Equation Decide

About 7 minutes

A straight line is great, but the slope is just a number with units until you know what it means. The slope isn't automatically g (or any other quantity). To find out what the slope means, use this method:

  1. Write the known equation that contains the variables from the lab. Use your equation sheet.
  2. Rearrange it so the vertical-axis variable is alone on the left side.
  3. Write the graph equation using the word slope:  (vertical variable) = slope · (horizontal variable) + b
  4. Line up the two equations and cross out what matches. Whatever is left over is what the slope means.

Example 1: Ball in free fall (released from rest)

Equation sheet x = x0 + vx0t + ½axt²
Released from rest, so vx0 = 0. Start measuring at x0 = 0. Call the distance fallen d, and the acceleration is g.
Known d = ½g · t²
Graph d = slope · t²  (graphing d vs. t², and b ≈ 0)

Example 2: Force and acceleration

Known F = m · a  (Newton's second law, F = net force)
Graph F = slope · a
See why we never use a letter for slope? In this experiment the slope is the mass m. If you used the letter m for slope too, one letter would stand for two different things in the same problem. Writing out the word slope keeps them separate.

Example 3: When the horizontal axis is changed

Known a = F · (1/m)
Graph a = slope · (1/m)  (graphing a vs. 1/m)

Watch out: units can fool you

Key ideaThe slope means whatever is left over when you line up the graph equation with the known equation. Never assume the slope is the quantity you're looking for. Always compare the equations.
Section 4

Best-Fit Lines and Finding the Slope

About 4 minutes

Real data never lands perfectly on a line. Every measurement has a little error. The best-fit line is your best estimate of the true relationship. Follow these rules:

  1. Draw one straight line with a ruler. Never connect the dots.
  2. Balance the points: about as many points above the line as below it.
  3. Don't force the line through the origin. Let the data decide where it goes.
  4. To find the slope, pick two far-apart points on the line that are NOT data points, and circle them.

      Quick Review

      Let's review the big ideas before your next try. Read each one, then answer the three practice questions.

      1. What linearizing is
      Change what you plot so the data makes a straight line. Your measurements don't change.
      2. Use the shape
      Squared → graph y vs. x². Inverse → graph y vs. 1/x. Square root → graph y² vs. x (or y vs. √x).
      3. Let the equation decide
      Write the graph equation with the word slope. Line it up with the known equation. Cross out what matches. What's left is the slope.
      4. Best-fit line
      One straight line, points balanced above and below. Find slope with two far-apart points on the line, not data points.
