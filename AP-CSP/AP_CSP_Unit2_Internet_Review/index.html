Station 1 · CED Topic 4.1

Networks & Paths

CSN-1.ACSN-1.B.2
Why this was tricky

Two things trip people up here. First, four words that sound alike: device, system, network, path. Second, questions that ask what is NOT needed, where the answer is the one that sounds most technical: "a single direct connection between two devices." Devices on the Internet are not wired straight to each other.

1Learn it

Computing device
A physical machine that can run a program. Examples: laptops, phones, servers, routers, smart sensors.
Computing system
A group of computing devices and programs working together for a common purpose.
Computing network
A group of interconnected computing devices that can send or receive data. A network is one type of computing system.
Path
The sequence of directly connected devices that starts at the sender and ends at the receiver.
Bandwidth
The maximum amount of data that can be sent in a fixed amount of time, usually measured in bits per second.

The big idea: devices aren't wired to each other

Your phone→Wi‑Fi router→ISP router→Router→Router→Cell tower→Friend's phone

Each arrow is one direct connection, and the whole chain is the path. Your phone connects directly to just one part of the Internet (a Wi‑Fi router or cell tower). There is no single wire from you to your friend. To get online at all, a device just needs to connect to something that's already on the Internet.

Memory hooks
  • Device = can it run a program? (A router can. A cable can't.)
  • System = devices + programs + a shared purpose.
  • Network = devices connected so they can send and receive data.
  • Path = the hops from sender to receiver.
  • Bandwidth = the max amount of data per second (bits per second).

Station 2 · CED Topic 4.1

Protocols, IP Addresses & Scalability

CSN-1.BCSN-1.C.4
Why this was tricky

The wrong answers sounded reasonable: "protocols are secret for security," "each company makes its own," "protocols are changed every year," "an IP address shows where a device was made." All four are false. Internet protocols are open and shared, and they were designed to scale.

1Learn it

Remember Lesson 3? Scheduling with your group without talking was chaos until everyone agreed on the same rules. That agreement is a protocol.

Protocol
An agreed-upon set of rules that specify the behavior of a system.
Open protocols
Internet protocols are open (nonproprietary): published for anyone to use and not owned by one company. That's why any company's device can connect.
IP address
The unique number assigned to each device on the Internet, so data can be sent to the right place.
Scalability
The capacity of a system to change in size to meet new demands. The Internet was designed to be scalable.

Open vs. closed

✓ How the Internet actually works
  • Protocols are open and published
  • Every device follows the same shared rules
  • Any company can build a device that connects
  • New devices join without changing the rules, so the Internet scales
✗ Trap answers
  • "Protocols are kept secret for security"
  • "Each company uses its own protocol"
  • "The government designs a protocol for each device"
  • "Protocols are rewritten as new devices join"

An IP address works like a mailing address

TO203.0.113.7
FROM198.51.100.24
…the data being sent…

Routers read the destination IP address to forward data toward the right device. The address has to be unique, or the data wouldn't know where to go. It says nothing about who made the device or where it was built.

Station 3 · CED Topics 4.1 & 4.2

Routing, Redundancy & Fault Tolerance

CSN-1.A.6CSN-1.B.5CSN-1.E
Why this was tricky

"Data always takes the shortest path" sounds logical, but Internet routing is dynamic, so data can take any available path. On minimum-cut questions, many students count only one device's connections, or stop at the first set of cuts that works. You need the minimum, so check both ends and the middle.

1Learn it

Routing
Finding a path from sender to receiver. On the Internet, routing is dynamic: it's not decided in advance, and different packets can take different paths.
Redundancy
Including extra components, like more than one path between two devices, so the system still works if some parts fail.
Fault tolerant
A system that can keep working even when parts fail. The Internet is fault tolerant because of redundancy.
The trade-off
Redundancy costs more (more equipment, money, and resources) but makes the network more reliable and helps it scale.
The minimum-cut method
  1. Count the sender's direct connections.
  2. Count the receiver's direct connections.
  3. Look for a narrow bridge in the middle, where only a few lines join two groups of devices.
  4. The smallest of these is your answer. Check it: after those cuts, can you still trace any path? If yes, keep looking.

Pro check: if you can find 3 paths that share no lines, you'll need at least 3 cuts, one per path.

2Try it: Network Lab

Tap a line to cut it (tap again to repair it). Press Send a packet to watch routing in action: packets pick different paths, and they reroute around cuts. Your goal: disconnect the sender from the receiver using the fewest cuts possible.

Station 4 · CED Topic 4.1

Packets, TCP & UDP

CSN-1.C
Why this was tricky

Trap answers said metadata is only on "important" packets, or travels in its own separate packet, or that packets always arrive in order, or that it's "all or nothing." In reality, every packet carries its own metadata, and packets can arrive in order, out of order, or not at all.

1Learn it

Data stream & packets
Information travels through the Internet as a data stream: chunks of data wrapped in packets. This happens to all data, big or small.
Packet metadata
Extra information on each packet, used to route it (sender and receiver IP addresses) and to reassemble the message (packet number).
Arrival
Each packet can take a different path, so packets may arrive in order, out of order, or not at all.

Anatomy of a packet

FROM 198.51.100.24TO   203.0.113.7PACKET 3 OF 6
VE OUT O
  • Metadata (gray): addresses tell routers where to send it. The packet number tells the receiver where it fits.
  • Data: one small chunk of the message. In the Internet Simulator, that was 8 characters.

Three protocols to know

ProtocolIts jobBest for
IPGives each device a unique address; each packet is addressed and routed toward its destination.Every packet on the Internet
TCPNumbers packets, checks they all arrived, puts them back in order, and requests any missing ones. Reliable but a bit slower.Web pages, email, downloads, photos: anything that must be complete and correct
UDPSends packets fast with no checking and no resending. Fast, but some data may be lost.Video calls, live streams, online games: when speed matters more than perfection

Station 5 · CED Topic 4.1

The Web, HTTP & DNS

CSN-1.DCSN-1.C.4
Why this was tricky

Protocol questions often list four similar-looking names. Give each one a job: HTTP = web pages, TCP = reliable packets, UDP = fast packets, IP = addresses. And remember that the Internet and the World Wide Web are not the same thing.

1Learn it

The Internet

The network itself: devices, routers, cables, Wi‑Fi, and the protocols that move data (IP, TCP, UDP).

The World Wide Web

A system of linked pages, programs, and files. The Web uses the Internet to share them, through HTTP.

Think of the Internet as the roads and the Web as one delivery service that uses them. Email, video calls, and online games also use the Internet, but they aren't the Web.

HTTP
HyperText Transfer Protocol: the protocol the Web uses to request and send pages and files. Your browser sends a GET request; the server sends the page back. HTTPS is the secure, encrypted version.
DNS
The Domain Name System translates names like code.org into IP addresses. It helps the Internet scale, so no one has to memorize billions of addresses. (From Code.org Lesson 6. The AP exam focuses on HTTP and on the Internet vs. the Web.)

Layers of the Internet: how they work together

HTTPAsks for the web page and sends it back (the Web)
TCP / UDPBreaks data into packets; TCP makes sure none are missing
IPAddresses every packet and routes it across the network
PhysicalWi‑Fi, cables, fiber, cell towers carry the bits

Station 6 · CED Topic 5.2

The Digital Divide

IOC-1.C
Why this was tricky

Several wrong answers were about technology problems: packets out of order, devices far apart, different brands of devices. The digital divide isn't about machines. It's about people having unequal access.

1Learn it

Digital divide
Differing access to computing devices and the Internet, based on socioeconomic, geographic, or demographic characteristics.
Socioeconomic
Money and income. Can a family afford a laptop or a monthly Internet plan?
Geographic
Location. Some rural areas have no high-speed Internet available at all.
Demographic
Groups of people. Access can differ by age, race or ethnicity, disability, and more.
  • It affects both individuals and groups, both locally and globally.
  • It raises issues of equity, access, and influence. People without access have less say in a world that runs online.
  • The actions of individuals, organizations, and governments can make it wider or narrower.
The PEOPLE + ACCESS test

Before choosing a digital divide answer, ask: (1) Is it about people? (2) Is it about unequal access to devices or the Internet? If an answer is about packets, routers, distance, or device brands, it's a distractor.

Red flag: anything important offered "online only" (voter registration, job applications, homework, permits) creates a digital divide problem for people without access.
